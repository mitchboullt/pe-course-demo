The Distribution Question

Who actually wins and who loses from PE — and is the distribution of outcomes justifiable?
activeconfidence: Highupdated 2026-05-2216 pieces of evidence

Working position

PE's incentive architecture systematically concentrates gains among financial participants (GPs via carry, LPs via fund returns, deal advisors via fees) while distributing costs to workers, patients, tenants, and taxpayers in specific sectors and crisis periods. This structural claim is now supported by seven independent evidence streams covering four decades. The employment evidence (Davis et al.) confirms both the positive productivity case (PE creates genuine efficiency) AND the distributional harm case (productivity gains appear to flow to investors, not workers; workers bear restructuring costs without sharing in equity upside). AIFMD's Article 26-30 proves that regulatory governance of the distributional impact on workers is achievable. KKR's worker equity experiment proves that the distribution is not inherent to PE's incentive architecture — it is a design choice. The normative question (is this distribution justifiable?) remains contested; the factual question (who bears what) is now well-evidenced.

The Regulatory Question

How do legislative and tax frameworks constrain and enable PE behavior — and are those frameworks adequate, or are they shaped by the industry they purport to govern?
activeconfidence: Highupdated 2026-05-2213 pieces of evidence

Working position

PE's regulatory environment has been systematically shaped by the industry it governs. The confirmed pattern: regulatory gaps enable PE behavior → behavior produces political salience → reform proposed → industry resistance blocks or weakens reform → partial regulatory response that addresses visible symptoms while leaving core behaviors intact. This pattern now has four complete documented iterations: FIRREA (1989) addressed S&L junk bond exposure while preserving high-yield; Dodd-Frank addressed PE regulatory invisibility while leaving leverage/carry/essential services untouched; 2023 SEC rules attempted LP protection and were vacated; AIFMD (EU) demonstrates that a more substantive framework is achievable. The key structural finding: the IAA's "clients are funds not LPs" architectural gap, confirmed judicially by the Fifth Circuit, means that SEC rulemaking cannot achieve comprehensive LP protection — this requires Congressional legislation. Crucially, AIFMD has now confirmed that the US regulatory inadequacy is a policy choice, not a technical constraint: the EU has operated a substantially more protective framework for over a decade without destroying its PE market.

The Historical Pattern

Is PE's development best understood as a recurring cycle of financial innovation → competitive excess → crisis → regulatory reform — and does understanding the cycle's mechanics let us predict where we are now?
activeconfidence: Highupdated 2026-05-2213 pieces of evidence

Working position

The historical evidence supports the pattern across three documented full iterations: 1989 junk bond collapse (Milken/Drexel/S&L crisis → FIRREA), 2008–2009 GFC (cov-lite/CLOs/peak leverage → Dodd-Frank), and the emerging 2022+ rate shock (NAV lending/subscription lines/floating-rate exposure → regulatory and market response in progress). Each iteration follows the same four stages, with variation in mechanism and severity. The GFC iteration revealed that PE can be an amplifier and victim of a crisis caused primarily by a parallel market (mortgages). The reform stage of each iteration follows the Regulatory Question thread's "form not substance" pattern — preserving core activity while constraining the specific excess. The most important sub-claim, now better evidenced: each reset creates conditions for the next cycle — near-zero rates and cov-lite normalisation post-GFC directly enabled the scale and leverage profiles that the 2022+ rate shock is now compressing. The PE and Employment note adds cross-cycle evidence: employment harm in each era is proportional to leverage levels — highly leveraged deals in each cycle produce concentrated employment costs, consistent with the pattern's prediction that excess produces harm.
