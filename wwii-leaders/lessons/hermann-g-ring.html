Biography 5 of 18

Hermann Göring

🎯 Predict First

  1. Göring was once Hitler's designated successor and one of the regime's most popular figures — what do you think caused his dramatic fall from favor?
  2. Was the Luftwaffe's failure to win the Battle of Britain mainly Göring's fault, or a deeper structural problem?
  3. What did Göring do at his own war-crimes trial that most defendants didn't?

📜 Snapshot

WWI flying ace, early Nazi Party member, founder of the Gestapo, commander-in-chief of the Luftwaffe, Hitler's officially designated successor for most of the war, tried at Nuremberg, sentenced to death, and died by suicide (smuggled cyanide) hours before his scheduled execution in 1946.

🔍 Beyond the Textbook Version

Göring's trajectory is a genuinely dramatic decline, and the "how" matters more than the pop-history shorthand of "fat, drug-addicted Nazi." In the 1920s-30s he was a legitimate WWI hero (a decorated fighter ace who commanded Manfred von Richthofen's former squadron) whose respectability helped make Nazism palatable to conservative elites Hitler needed. Through the mid-1930s he built the Luftwaffe into a genuinely formidable force and ran a wide portfolio (economic mobilization via the Four-Year Plan, the Gestapo's founding, aggressive art looting across occupied Europe) that made him the regime's most powerful figure after Hitler.

The Luftwaffe's failure to win the Battle of Britain (1940) is the real inflection point. Göring made specific, consequential command errors — most significantly shifting the bombing campaign's focus from RAF airfields and radar infrastructure (which were close to breaking the RAF's operational capacity) to London itself in September 1940, a change partly driven by a desire for retaliation after an RAF raid on Berlin and partly by overconfidence in the Luftwaffe's ability to force capitulation through terror bombing — inadvertently giving Fighter Command time to recover. His subsequent promise to keep the encircled German 6th Army supplied by air at Stalingrad (winter 1942-43) was a catastrophic overreach the Luftwaffe never had the airlift capacity to fulfill, contributing directly to one of the war's most decisive German defeats. Each failure eroded his standing with Hitler, and by the war's final years he had largely retreated from active leadership into isolation at his estate, addicted to morphine (originally prescribed after a WWI-era wound) and absorbed in looted art collecting rather than military administration — Hitler formally stripped him of all offices and expelled him from the party in the war's final days, after Göring's attempt to assume leadership (interpreting Hitler's isolation in the Berlin bunker as effective incapacitation) was read as an attempted coup.

🧭 How They Operated

Göring accumulated an extraordinary number of overlapping official titles and personal luxuries — private trains, a lavish hunting estate, an enormous uniform collection — that became a genuine source of internal Nazi ridicule even as he remained publicly popular with ordinary Germans for longer than most other senior figures, thanks partly to residual WWI-hero credibility and a more affable public persona than Himmler's or Goebbels's. This personal indulgence tracked his declining operational engagement: as the war turned against Germany, contemporary accounts describe him increasingly absent from serious military planning.

📰 Primary Source

At his Nuremberg trial, Göring was widely noted by observers (including American prosecutor Robert Jackson, who found him a far more formidable cross-examination opponent than expected) for aggressively defending Nazi policy and his own role rather than expressing remorse or distancing himself from the regime — a marked contrast to defendants like Speer, who cultivated an image of technocratic remove from the regime's crimes. Göring reportedly told a prison psychologist that he intended to go down in history either as a great man or as a villain who at least remained loyal to his cause, rather than as someone who groveled for leniency.

❗ Common Misconceptions

  • "Göring was always a marginal, buffoonish figure."** For roughly the first decade of the regime he was among its most powerful and effective administrators — the decline came specifically from the mid-war military failures described above, not from constant incompetence.
  • "The Battle of Britain was lost mainly due to German technical or numerical inferiority."** The RAF's radar network and Fighter Command organization mattered enormously, but Göring's specific decision to shift bombing away from airfields to cities in September 1940 is widely treated by historians as a major unforced strategic error that gave the RAF crucial breathing room.

❓ Test Yourself

Q1. What specific command decision in September 1940 is widely blamed for helping the RAF survive the Battle of Britain?

Shifting the Luftwaffe's bombing focus from RAF airfields and radar infrastructure to London itself, which relieved pressure on Fighter Command's operational capacity right as it was nearing a breaking point.

Q2. What promise did Göring make regarding Stalingrad, and how did it turn out?

He promised to keep the encircled 6th Army supplied entirely by air; the Luftwaffe never had the capacity to fulfill this, contributing to the catastrophic German defeat there.

Q3. How did Göring's approach at his Nuremberg trial differ from many other defendants?

He aggressively defended Nazi policy and his own role rather than expressing remorse or distancing himself, in contrast to figures like Speer who cultivated an image of technocratic distance from the regime's crimes.
