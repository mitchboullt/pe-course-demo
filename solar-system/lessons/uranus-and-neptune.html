Uranus and Neptune

🔭 Predict First

  1. Uranus and Neptune are called "ice giants," distinct from Jupiter and Saturn's "gas giants" — what do you think the difference is?
  2. One planet in the solar system rotates almost completely on its side. Which one do you think it is?
  3. Which planet do you think has the fastest winds in the entire solar system?

🪐 The Big Picture

Uranus and Neptune are the solar system's ice giants — smaller and colder than Jupiter and Saturn, made of a different mix of materials, and each holding its own record: Uranus for the most extreme axial tilt of any planet, and Neptune for the fastest winds ever measured anywhere in the solar system.

🔬 How It Works

Unlike Jupiter and Saturn, which are mostly hydrogen and helium gas, Uranus and Neptune have a much higher proportion of heavier "ices" — water, ammonia, and methane — swirling under intense pressure beneath a hydrogen-helium outer atmosphere. This different composition earns them the separate category "ice giants" rather than "gas giants," even though neither one has anything you could stand on. Methane in their upper atmospheres absorbs red light and reflects blue-green light back, which is why both planets appear distinctly blue.

Uranus is famous for an extreme axial tilt of about 98 degrees — it essentially rotates on its side, rolling around the Sun like a ball rather than spinning like a top the way most planets do. Nobody knows for certain why, but the leading hypothesis is an ancient massive collision that knocked it over. This tilt gives Uranus the strangest seasons in the solar system: each pole gets about 42 years of continuous sunlight, followed by 42 years of continuous darkness, over its 84-year orbit.

Neptune, meanwhile, holds the wind-speed record for the entire solar system: winds have been measured exceeding 2,100 km/h — faster than the speed of sound on Earth — even though Neptune receives less than half the sunlight (and therefore less than half the energy input) that Uranus does. Exactly why Neptune's winds are so much faster than Uranus's, despite the two planets being so similar in size and composition, remains an open question in planetary science.

🚀 Mission Spotlight

Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to have ever visited Uranus (1986) or Neptune (1989) — no dedicated mission to either ice giant has launched since, making Voyager 2's decades-old flyby data still the primary source of close-up information scientists have about both planets.

📊 By The Numbers

  • Uranus: distance 19.2 AU · year ~84 Earth years · day ~17 hours · axial tilt ~98° · moons 29** confirmed (as of 2026)
  • Neptune: distance 30.1 AU · year ~165 Earth years · day ~16 hours · fastest winds 2,100+ km/h · moons 18** confirmed (as of 2026)

❗ Common Misconceptions

  • "Uranus and Neptune are just smaller versions of Jupiter and Saturn."** Their composition is meaningfully different — more water/ammonia/methane "ice" relative to hydrogen and helium — which is exactly why they get their own category, "ice giants."
  • "The planet closer to the Sun should have the stronger weather, since it gets more energy."** Neptune's winds are far faster than Uranus's despite receiving under half the sunlight — a genuine open puzzle, not something energy input alone explains.

❓ Test Yourself

Q1. What makes Uranus and Neptune "ice giants" rather than "gas giants" like Jupiter and Saturn?

A higher proportion of heavier "ices" (water, ammonia, methane) relative to hydrogen and helium.

Q2. What's unusual about Uranus's axial tilt, and what effect does it have?

It's tilted about 98°, essentially rotating on its side — this gives each pole about 42 years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 years of darkness.

Q3. Which planet has the fastest winds in the solar system?

Neptune — over 2,100 km/h, despite receiving less sunlight than Uranus.

📚 New Terms

Ice giant
a giant planet made largely of water, ammonia, and methane ices beneath a hydrogen-helium atmosphere (Uranus and Neptune).
Axial tilt
the angle between a planet's rotation axis and its orbital plane.
