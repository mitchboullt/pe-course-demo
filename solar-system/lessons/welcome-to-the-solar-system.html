🪐 The Big Picture

The solar system is the Sun and everything gravitationally bound to it: eight planets, at least five recognized dwarf planets, hundreds of moons, and countless asteroids, comets, and dust — all held in orbit by the Sun's gravity, which makes up 99.8% of all the mass in the entire system.

🔬 How It Works

The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a giant, slowly rotating cloud of gas and dust called the solar nebula. Gravity pulled most of the material inward into a dense, hot center that became the Sun. The leftover material flattened into a spinning disk, and within that disk, dust grains stuck together, then clumped into rocks, then into planet-sized bodies — a process called accretion.

Where you ended up in that disk determined what kind of planet you became. Close to the young Sun, it was too hot for ice or gas to survive, so only rock and metal could condense — that's why Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are small, dense, rocky worlds. Beyond a boundary called the frost line (roughly where the asteroid belt is today), it was cold enough for ice to form, giving those growing planets a huge head start in mass — enough gravity to grab onto massive amounts of hydrogen and helium gas straight from the disk. That's why Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune are enormous gas and ice giants.

The solar system has a rough structure moving outward from the Sun: the four rocky inner planets, the asteroid belt, the four giant outer planets, then the icy Kuiper Belt, and finally the vast, distant Oort Cloud that may extend nearly a light-year from the Sun.

🚀 Mission Spotlight

Voyager 1 and 2, launched in 1977, are the only spacecraft to have visited all four giant planets between them. Voyager 1 is now the most distant human-made object ever built — as of 2026 it's over 160 AU from the Sun (more than 160 times the Earth-Sun distance) and still sending back data from interstellar space, decades past its original mission.

📊 By The Numbers

  • The Sun contains about 99.8% of the solar system's total mass.
  • The solar system is roughly 4.6 billion years old.
  • There are 8 official planets, 5 recognized dwarf planets, and well over 400 confirmed moons across the giant planets alone (and rising every year as astronomers find more).
  • The Oort Cloud may extend up to 100,000 AU from the Sun — almost a light-year away.

❗ Common Misconceptions

  • "The solar system's planets are lined up close together like in most diagrams."** Real diagrams almost always compress distance to fit on a page. In true scale, if Earth were a marble, Neptune would be roughly a mile away, and the space between planets would be almost entirely empty. Distance is one of the hardest things about space to picture honestly.
  • "The asteroid belt is so packed with rocks that a spaceship would have to dodge them constantly."** In reality the asteroid belt is mostly empty space — spacecraft have flown through it many times without incident, because the average distance between asteroids is millions of kilometers.

❓ Test Yourself

Q1. What is the "frost line," and why did it matter for how planets formed?

The frost line is the distance from the Sun beyond which it was cold enough for ice to survive. Planets that formed beyond it could accumulate much more mass (rock + ice), letting their gravity pull in huge amounts of gas — producing giant planets, versus small rocky ones closer in.

Q2. About what percentage of the solar system's mass is in the Sun?

About 99.8%.

Q3. Put these in order from the Sun outward: Kuiper Belt, inner rocky planets, Oort Cloud, outer giant planets, asteroid belt.

Inner rocky planets → asteroid belt → outer giant planets → Kuiper Belt → Oort Cloud.

📚 New Terms

Solar nebula
the giant cloud of gas and dust the whole solar system formed from.
Accretion
the process of small particles sticking together and growing into larger bodies like planets.
Frost line
the distance from the Sun beyond which ices could survive during planet formation.
Astronomical Unit (AU)
the average Earth-Sun distance (about 150 million km), used as a convenient unit for solar system distances.
