Earth and the Moon

🔭 Predict First

  1. What do scientists think formed the Moon: was it captured by Earth's gravity, or did it form some other way?
  2. Does the Moon always show the same face to Earth, or does it rotate freely like Earth does?
  3. What causes ocean tides here on Earth?

🪐 The Big Picture

Earth is the only known world with life, liquid surface water, and a breathable oxygen atmosphere — and it has an unusually large moon for its size, formed in a catastrophic collision that also happens to explain why the Moon always shows Earth the same face.

🔬 How It Works

The leading explanation for the Moon's origin is the giant-impact hypothesis: about 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized protoplanet (often called Theia) collided with the young Earth. The collision blasted a huge amount of debris into orbit, which gradually gathered together under its own gravity to form the Moon. This explains several odd facts at once — like why the Moon's composition closely resembles Earth's outer layers, and why the Moon is so unusually large relative to its parent planet compared to almost every other moon in the solar system.

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth: it rotates on its axis in exactly the same time it takes to orbit Earth (about 27.3 days), so it always shows Earth the same face — the famous "dark side of the Moon" isn't actually dark, it just never faces us. Tidal locking happens because Earth's gravity pulls slightly harder on the near side of the Moon than the far side, creating a gravitational drag that, over billions of years, has slowed the Moon's rotation until it matched its orbit exactly.

The Moon's gravity is also the main cause of Earth's ocean tides: it pulls the ocean water on the side of Earth facing the Moon into a bulge, while inertia creates a second bulge on the opposite side — as Earth rotates through these two bulges each day, most coastlines experience roughly two high tides and two low tides.

🚀 Mission Spotlight

The Apollo program (1969–1972) landed 12 astronauts on the Moon and returned about 382 kg of lunar rock and soil samples — still being studied today, and the direct evidence that confirmed key parts of the giant-impact hypothesis by revealing how similar lunar rock composition is to Earth's mantle.

📊 By The Numbers

  • Earth's distance from the Sun: 1 AU (about 150 million km) — the definition of an AU.
  • Earth's orbital period: 365.25 days.
  • The Moon's distance from Earth: about 384,000 km.
  • The Moon's orbital/rotation period: 27.3 days (tidally locked).
  • The Moon is about 1/4 of Earth's diameter — unusually large for a moon relative to its planet.

❗ Common Misconceptions

  • "There's a permanently dark side of the Moon."** Every part of the Moon gets sunlight at some point during its 27.3-day cycle — what's true is that one side (the far side) never faces Earth, which is different from "always dark."
  • "Tides happen just because the Moon 'pulls' the ocean toward it."** That's half the story — there's a second tidal bulge on the far side of Earth too, caused by inertia, which is why most places get two high tides a day, not one.

❓ Test Yourself

Q1. What is the leading hypothesis for how the Moon formed?

The giant-impact hypothesis: a Mars-sized body collided with early Earth, and the resulting debris coalesced into the Moon.

Q2. Why does the Moon always show Earth the same face?

It's tidally locked — its rotation period exactly matches its orbital period around Earth.

Q3. Why do most coastlines get roughly two high tides a day, not just one?

There are two tidal bulges — one on the side facing the Moon (direct gravitational pull) and one on the opposite side (caused by inertia) — and Earth rotates through both each day.

📚 New Terms

Giant-impact hypothesis
the leading theory that the Moon formed from debris after a Mars-sized body collided with early Earth.
Tidally locked
when a body's rotation period exactly matches its orbital period, so it always shows the same face to what it orbits.

🔗 Related Concepts

  • Venus — Earth's true "twin" in size, but a hostile contrast in habitability.
  • Mars — the next rocky planet outward, and another candidate in the search for past life.
