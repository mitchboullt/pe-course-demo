Orbits and Kepler's Laws

🔭 Predict First

  1. Do you think planets move at a constant speed all the way around their orbit, or does their speed change?
  2. Is a planet's orbit shaped like a perfect circle, or something else?
  3. If Planet A is twice as far from the Sun as Planet B, do you think Planet A takes exactly twice as long to orbit, or more than twice as long?

🪐 The Big Picture

Everything orbiting the Sun follows three precise mathematical rules discovered by German astronomer Johannes Kepler in the early 1600s — rules so accurate they still describe real orbits today, from planets to spacecraft to exoplanets around other stars.

🔬 How It Works

Kepler's First Law: orbits aren't perfect circles — they're ellipses (ovals), with the Sun sitting at one of the two focus points of the ellipse, not the center. Most planetary orbits are close to circular, but comets and some asteroids can have wildly stretched-out ellipses.

Kepler's Second Law: a planet doesn't move at constant speed. An imaginary line connecting the planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times — which means a planet moves faster when it's closer to the Sun (near perihelion, its closest point) and slower when it's farther away (near aphelion, its farthest point). This isn't arbitrary — it directly follows from the physics of gravity and momentum.

Kepler's Third Law is the one you can actually calculate with: it says the square of a planet's orbital period (T) is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun (a). In the special case where T is measured in Earth years and a is measured in AU, the law simplifies to a clean equation:

T² = a³

This single equation connects any orbiting body's distance to its orbital period — which is exactly why it's useful far beyond just the eight planets: it works for asteroids, comets, and even moons orbiting other planets (with the right constant swapped in).

🚀 Mission Spotlight

Mission planners use Kepler's laws every day. A Hohmann transfer orbit — the fuel-efficient path most spacecraft use to travel between planets — is literally half of an elliptical orbit, calculated directly from Kepler's equations. NASA's Perseverance rover reached Mars via exactly this kind of orbit-based trajectory planning.

📊 By The Numbers

  • Earth's orbit is very close to circular: distance from the Sun varies only from about 147 million km (perihelion) to 152 million km (aphelion).
  • Kepler discovered these laws using only naked-eye observations recorded by his mentor Tycho Brahe — decades before Newton explained why they work, using gravity.
  • Kepler's Third Law (T² = a³ in years/AU) predicts Neptune's ~165-year orbit from its ~30.1 AU distance almost exactly.

❗ Common Misconceptions

  • "Planets move at the same speed all the way around their orbit."** They speed up near the Sun and slow down far from it — this is Kepler's Second Law, and it's a real, measurable effect, not a rounding error.
  • "An ellipse just means 'a slightly squished circle, roughly centered on the Sun.'"** The Sun sits at one focus of the ellipse — offset from the geometric center, not in the middle. For most planets this offset is small, but for comets it can be huge.

❓ Test Yourself

Q1. State Kepler's Second Law in your own words.

A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times as it orbits — meaning it moves fastest when closest to the Sun (perihelion) and slowest when farthest (aphelion).

Q2. What shape is a planetary orbit, and where does the Sun sit within that shape?

An ellipse, with the Sun at one of the two focus points (not the center).

Q3. Write Kepler's Third Law as an equation (using years and AU).

T² = a³ (T = orbital period in Earth years, a = average distance from the Sun in AU).

🧮 Work The Problem

Worked example: Mars orbits at an average distance of a = 1.52 AU. What's its orbital period?

Using T² = a³:
T² = (1.52)³ = 3.51
T = √3.51 ≈ 1.87 years

(Mars's real measured orbital period is 687 days ≈ 1.88 years — Kepler's simple equation nails it almost exactly.)

Your turn: Jupiter orbits at an average distance of a = 5.20 AU. Using the same method:

  1. Calculate a³.
  2. Take the square root to find T in years.
  3. Compare your answer to Jupiter's real orbital period (~11.86 years) — how close did you get?
a³ = (5.20)³ = 140.6
T = √140.6 ≈ 11.86 years — matching the real value almost exactly.

📚 New Terms

Ellipse
an oval shape with two focus points; the shape of every orbit.
Focus (of an ellipse)
one of two special points inside an ellipse; the Sun sits at one of them for planetary orbits.
Perihelion
the closest point in an orbit to the Sun.
Aphelion
the farthest point in an orbit from the Sun.
Orbital period
the time it takes to complete one full orbit.
